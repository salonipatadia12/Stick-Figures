## The real origin of crude oil

### A

1. Is the oil you burn really dinosaurs?
2. You probably think crude oil is sea monsters or dinosaurs sinking into swamps.
3. Oil worth drilling for needs specific elements and processes inside a sedimentary basin.
4. The leading theory says oil comes from the remains of organic material.
5. Deeper than ten meters, anaerobic bacteria no longer act, so organic matter becomes kerogen.
6. Crude oil forms in mudrocks packed with organic matter and the right kind of kerogen.
7. Those remains were ancient marine animals and plants, from before dinosaurs.
8. So oil is ancient marine remains, turned to kerogen in mudrock, then thermally converted.
9. So no, oil was never melted dinosaurs.

### B

1. Before it was crude, what was it?
2. Many think a dinosaur sank in a swamp and became the oil in an oil well.
3. Not quite. The real story starts somewhere stranger.
4. The birthplace is mudrock, rich in organic matter and holding the right kind of kerogen.
5. Scientists think that deeper than 10 meters, bacteria stop working and organic matter turns into kerogen.
6. Oil worth drilling needs every ingredient and step to happen inside a sedimentary basin.
7. So where does it start? The leading theory says oil forms from remains of once-living things.
8. Most geochemists believe the evidence overwhelmingly backs petroleum coming from life.
9. Its source was ancient sea animals and plants, which were around before dinosaurs even existed.
10. Most experts think buried marine plants and animals become kerogen, then heat turns that into oil.
11. Next time you see crude, think about what it was before.

## Why do we yawn?

### A

1. Why can an unseen watcher stop a yawn?
2. Many think yawning is contagious or boredom, just a mouth habit with no brain purpose.
3. That story starts to crack when you look closer.
4. Look first at the act itself: a deep breath in, on purpose, mouth stretched wide.
5. The command starts in the hypothalamus, then travels through the brainstem down to the spinal cord.
6. Chemical messengers like dopamine, serotonin and oxytocin help steer it, along with particular nerve pathways.
7. Newborns' brain and eye area cooled 0.36°C within 10 to 20 seconds of yawning.
8. Scientists haven't found that a yawn means you need sleep, or that low oxygen causes it.
9. When your jaw stretches wide, warm blood slips out of the skull and cools the brain.
10. Yawning likely cools the brain, as a wide jaw and altered breathing boost facial circulation.
11. Next time yawning hits, you'll know what your head is doing.

### B

1. You yawn five to twenty times daily. Why?
2. It's a deliberate deep breath with your mouth stretched wide.
3. Scientists haven't found that it means you're sleepy or short on oxygen.
4. What it does change is your breathing rate, which boosts blood circulation in your face.
5. And opening your jaw wide lets warm blood escape from the skull.
6. In newborns, the head and eye area cooled about 0.36°C within 10 to 20 seconds of yawning.
7. So yawning likely cools the brain, using a wide jaw and altered breathing to release warm blood.
8. So catch your next yawn, and notice how wide your jaw opens.

8. That's what the Roman Republic fell from: its own success.

## How compound interest works

### A

1. Why was interest math once a secret?
2. Most of us assume interest only counts your first deposit.
3. So what was everyone missing?
4. Then Fibonacci, in 1202, developed techniques for it, but they needed a great deal of laborious arithmetic.
5. The twist: interest you've already earned counts too, not just your savings.
6. You earn on both, so the longer your money stays in savings, the more you earn.
7. No wonder some people guarded it like a secret.

### B

1. How does compound interest turn tiny into huge?
2. Many think your money's growth comes only from your first deposit.
3. That picture misses something, and it's a big one.
4. Leave your money in a savings account for more years, and compound interest pays you more.
5. Wait, that sounds too good to be true.
6. Here's the kicker: savings grow even faster with more frequent compounding, a higher rate, or added principal.
7. You earn on your original savings and the interest already earned, so growth accelerates, especially over longer periods.
8. Tiny isn't tiny for long, once compound interest gets going.

## Why the sky is blue

### A

1. How does colorless air make a blue sky?
2. Many people think the atmosphere is a mirror, bouncing blue light down from space.
3. Sunlight enters our atmosphere, and tiny air molecules scatter it in every direction.
4. So why would blue get picked out?
5. Here's the clue: oxygen and nitrogen are small enough to work best on shorter wavelengths.
6. Blue travels in shorter waves, so it scatters more than other colors.
7. Sunlight looks white, but its blue gets scattered to your eyes while red keeps going straight.
8. That selective scattering by air molecules is what makes a clear, sunny sky blue.
9. So look up: that sky is air scattering blue.

### B

1. How did plain sunlight become blue sky?
2. Many think the atmosphere simply reflects blue sunlight down from space like a mirror.
3. That picture falls apart once you look closer.
4. Most of the air around you is nitrogen and oxygen, and they bounce sunlight around.
5. Small floating particles toss short waves around much more than long ones.
6. Blue light gets knocked around by those molecules more strongly than red light.
7. Which means every cloudless sunny afternoon, those air molecules are the reason you're looking up at blue.
8. Sunlight looks white, but its blue parts get flung toward you while red sails on.
9. Tiny molecules scatter sunlight's shorter blue wavelengths more strongly than red, sending blue across the sky.
10. Look up now, and that blue is no longer a mystery.
